## Description of Design
This is a multiplier that sequentially (even though this is technically hardware and not code) follows Booth's algorithm to complete multiplication between a multiplicand (dataoperandA) and a multiplier (dataoperandB). The initial term that gets loaded in is {32b'0, multiplier/dataoperandB, 1'b0}. The result that emerges after completing booths algorithm is stored in a register that updates only once one cycle of Booths has been applied. I use a mux to determine which operation I will do based on the last 2 bits of the entire 65-bit term. I also compute the 2s complement of the multiplicand, and then will send the correct term (A, ~A, or 32'b0) to a hardcoded adder to complete the addition. Finally, I will look at the 33 LSB, exclude the added 1st LSB, to be looking at the numerical answer of booths, which is bits [32:1] of my register. For overflow, I check to see if I have any specific cases where the inputs are the combination of -1 and the smallest possible input, which will trigger an overflow. I also check to see if the uppermost 32 bits of the register [64:33] are the correct sign extension of the beginning portion of the register [32:1], which will trigger an overflow if not the same.

The divider follows the non-resorting algorithm as seen in lecture. Using the same modules as I used in the multiplier, in addition to a new counter and smaller 64 bit register, I worked through the flowchart depicting what operations to do. Unlike multiplication, though, I first had to ensure that both the divisor and dividend were positive, which I was able to do by analyzing the sign bits of the respective pairs and selecting the positive version (converting always, but not always passing the conversion through). Then, I concatenated 32 0's with the Dividend. Then I used this stored value to analyze the sign bit of A to determine if I should add or subtract the divisor from A (following a left shift). Following this addition (of either M or ~M) and reconcatenation with the dividend, I once again looked at the updated sign but of A, and changed the LSB of the updated AQ stored term to be ~sign bit. Unlike in the diagram, where the dividend could be N bits, in our case, we always know that the dividend and the divsors will be 32 bits, so I implemented a counter very similar to the counter in my multipler, but since this time I was able to communicate the finished division quotient in 32 cycles (as opposed to 33 with multiplcation), so ran through one less count. I also counted upwards from 0 until 31 (as opposed to downwards from 31) because of this forced constraint of 32 bits. When the algorithm was finished, and since we did not care to know what the remainder of the operation was, we could skip directly to extracting the Quotient from the 32 LSBs of the stored (but continuously updated) register.

Combining these two modules in the mult div file simply comes down to reserving wires for the outputs of each operation, multiplication and division, and assigning those wires to be communicated and released when the correct operation is on. This is done through a DFF, which terminates operations when the operations are done, and uses a mux to determine whether to send the multiplication or division results depending on the control inputs.

## Bugs
I have no bugs in my final work. While I was building, I ecountered two major bugs. The first bug was purley algorithmic errors. I was misreading the slides on how to apply booths algoirthm, and I was trying to shift the initial term (32b'0, operandB, 0) instead of applying booths algoirthm to it first and then shifting. This was causing some of my final answers to be off by +-operandA. It wasn't until I asked a clarifying question that I realized my approach was incorrect. The second error that caused some frustration was endinga cycle too early. When I was finished implementing the correct algorithm, I noticed that many of my answers were still double the expected answer, meaning that I was off by one arithmetic right shift. I used gtkwave to examine the results of my work, and I noticed that because I only loaded in operands on the first cycle, I was outputting my answer one arithmetic shift too early, which was messing up my final answer. Upon extending my counter to 6 bits instead of 5, my bug was fixed.

Implementing the division module was straightforward, since I had spent so much time debugging multiplication, I was very comfortable with how to manipulate the algorithm to fit non-restoring division. I had small (duh-type) bugs when I implemented division, and then combined division and multiplication to form multdi.v. I did have one bug with not passing one hidden test case, but I fixed it by reconsidering what edge cases I needed to check for overflow in multiplication (I was passing through false positives based on my overflow module with the cases of the minimum negative times 1 = minimum negative, and vice versa). This does not lead to an overflow, but my overflow module was counting it as an overflow because the 32 MSBs are all 0s, but the sign bit is 1. This caused it to be marked as an overflow, since the sign bit does not match the rest of the MSB, but it should not have been marked as an overflow since, obviously, the product can fit in the allotted number of bits.
